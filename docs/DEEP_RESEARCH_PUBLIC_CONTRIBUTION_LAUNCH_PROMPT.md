# Deep Research Prompt: Public Contribution Network for Historical Handwriting

## Research assignment

I need a rigorous launch and growth strategy for a public volunteer project that helps
transcribe historical handwritten records. Research this as an actual product and movement—not
merely a website or SEO exercise.

## Project context

I am developing an open-source project currently called **AKTREADER**. Its purpose is to create
a free, auditable alternative to expensive historical-handwriting extraction tools.

The initial material is 19th- and early-20th-century civil-register handwriting from Congress
Poland, especially Serock and Pułtusk. Documents may use pre-1917 Russian Cyrillic or Polish and
contain births, marriages, deaths, names, relationships, dates, occupations, and marginal
corrections.

The system combines:

- local/offline vision-language models;
- exact source-image provenance;
- uncertainty and abstention instead of confident guessing;
- independent readings and disagreement detection;
- human review as the final authority;
- open benchmarking and, when separately authorized, model training.

I have already built a pilot containing small, image-based tasks. A visitor might:

- type a short word or line exactly as seen;
- compare two possible readings;
- decide whether repeated name shapes match;
- verify an act number;
- select “neither” or “can’t tell”;
- optionally identify relevant language or paleography experience.

Visitors must not see model guesses or previous answers before submitting. Crowd consensus must
not automatically become historical truth. Submissions are evidence used for corroboration,
calibration, disagreement detection, and routing to qualified readers.

My personal portfolio is `jacobgoldstein.dev`, but I am concerned that placing the project only
there will produce little discovery, trust, participation, or institutional interest.
**Itinera is somebody else’s project and must not be used or implied as my name or affiliation.**
AKTREADER and CyrillicApp are working names, not irrevocable brand decisions.

## Core research question

What is the highest-impact way to launch, brand, host, distribute, and grow this project so that
it attracts useful volunteers, expert readers, genealogy communities, archival institutions,
open-source contributors, and public attention?

Do not assume that SEO or a standalone domain is necessarily the answer. Compare all realistic
approaches and recommend one.

## Research requirements

### 1. Distribution and positioning

Compare:

- a section of `jacobgoldstein.dev`;
- a subdomain;
- an independent project domain;
- a GitHub-centered open-source project;
- a hosted citizen-science platform;
- partnerships with existing genealogy, archive, university, Jewish-history, Polish-history,
  or transcription communities;
- a hybrid arrangement.

Evaluate credibility, discoverability, contributor conversion, search authority,
maintainability, institutional acceptance, and long-term brand independence.

Explain whether a standalone domain would materially help or merely create an empty site with
no authority.

### 2. Comparable projects

Study successful and unsuccessful historical-transcription or citizen-science projects,
including relevant examples such as Zooniverse, FamilySearch indexing, FromThePage, Smithsonian
Transcription Center, DigiVol, Europeana projects, Transkribus communities, and smaller
volunteer-led projects.

For each useful comparison, identify:

- how contributors discovered it;
- what motivated them;
- task length and difficulty;
- onboarding design;
- whether accounts were required;
- feedback and retention mechanisms;
- quality-control systems;
- expert escalation;
- community features;
- institutional partnerships;
- what appears to have failed or limited participation.

Extract patterns applicable to a small independent project without an existing institution or
audience.

### 3. Audience and acquisition

Identify and prioritize realistic contributor groups:

- amateur and professional genealogists;
- Jewish genealogy communities;
- Polish genealogy and regional-history groups;
- Russian and Polish speakers;
- paleographers and archivists;
- history and Slavic-language students;
- citizen-science participants;
- open-source developers;
- descendants connected to Serock or Pułtusk;
- retirees or hobbyists who enjoy document puzzles.

Find active communities, organizations, forums, subreddits, Facebook groups, mailing lists,
societies, university departments, conferences, newsletters, and relevant creators.

For each promising channel, provide:

- current URL;
- approximate relevance and activity;
- community rules concerning promotion or research recruitment;
- the appropriate pitch;
- whether I should ask moderators first;
- expected value and reputational risk.

Do not recommend indiscriminate posting or spam.

### 4. Naming and domain strategy

Develop 15–25 credible project names. The name should communicate some combination of historical
records, handwriting, open access, human participation, evidence, and recovery of names from
archives.

Evaluate AKTREADER, CyrillicApp, “Help Read History,” and new alternatives.

For the strongest names:

- check current domain availability across sensible extensions;
- search for existing products, organizations, trademarks, or confusingly similar projects;
- assess pronunciation, memorability, international use, and SEO value;
- distinguish the technical product name from the public volunteer campaign if appropriate.

Do not recommend Itinera.

### 5. Volunteer experience

Design the ideal first-time contributor flow for a task completed in 30–90 seconds.

Specify:

- landing-page headline and explanation;
- trust signals;
- manuscript crop presentation;
- zoom, contrast, and accessibility controls;
- question types;
- “neither” and “can’t tell” behavior;
- optional expertise questions;
- optional attribution;
- completion feedback;
- progress and impact reporting;
- return-visitor mechanisms;
- advanced lanes for qualified readers.

Explain how to make participation emotionally meaningful without exaggerating historical
certainty.

### 6. Quality, ethics, and consent

Design a credible contribution policy that separates:

- use in historical research;
- release in an open benchmark;
- use in AI training;
- public attribution.

These permissions must not be bundled.

Address:

- blind independent submissions;
- calibration tasks with known answers;
- contributor reliability measurement;
- expert adjudication;
- correlated crowd errors;
- spam and malicious input;
- privacy and unnecessary personal-data collection;
- source-image copyright and archival terms;
- preservation of uncertainty;
- transparent correction and appeal procedures.

Recommend exactly which data fields should accompany every submission.

### 7. SEO and public knowledge strategy

Determine whether SEO can realistically produce contributors in the first year.

Develop an evidence-based content strategy around genuinely useful resources such as:

- reading pre-1917 Russian handwriting;
- understanding Polish/Russian civil registers;
- historical Cyrillic letterforms;
- genealogy transcription methods;
- AI failure modes in archival handwriting;
- Serock and Pułtusk register guides;
- open-source genealogy OCR;
- project discoveries and benchmark reports.

Separate pages that should be indexed from thin task pages or raw user submissions that should
not.

Do not invent keyword volumes. Cite actual data where available and label estimates clearly.

### 8. Launch economics and execution

Provide three launch plans:

- $0 volunteer-first;
- maximum $250;
- maximum $1,000.

Include domain, hosting, anti-spam, form storage, design, outreach, expert review, and modest
promotional spending where justified.

Identify what should not be purchased yet.

### 9. Impact measurement

Design a measurable funnel:

**visitor → starts task → submits task → submits another task → becomes reliable contributor →
becomes qualified specialist**

Recommend first-month, 90-day, and one-year targets for:

- unique contributors;
- completed tasks;
- returning contributors;
- abstention rate;
- agreement and disagreement;
- expert-reviewed fields;
- image-attested benchmark records;
- cost per usable contribution;
- mailing-list or community growth.

Distinguish vanity metrics from evidence that the project is producing trustworthy historical
data.

## Final deliverable

Produce:

1. A blunt executive verdict explaining the best launch architecture.
2. A decision matrix comparing personal-site section, subdomain, independent domain, hosted
   citizen-science platform, partnership model, and hybrid.
3. A recommended brand structure and five strongest names.
4. Verified domain and naming-conflict research.
5. A detailed audience/channel table with live links and outreach rules.
6. The ideal contributor flow and landing-page copy.
7. A quality-control, consent, and provenance framework.
8. A 90-day launch plan broken down week by week.
9. $0, $250, and $1,000 budget plans.
10. A one-year SEO and content roadmap.
11. A list of major risks and how to mitigate them.
12. The five highest-leverage actions I should take first.

Use current sources and include direct citations near every factual claim. Prefer primary
sources, official community rules, and observable current activity. Clearly distinguish sourced
facts, estimates, and strategic inference.

Be skeptical. If the concept is unlikely to attract sustained participation without an
institutional partner, say so and explain the best partnership route. If an independent domain
is justified, explain what would make it succeed rather than merely exist.
